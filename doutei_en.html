道程 (Dōtei)

Journey

TAKAMURA Kōtarō

I am not walking

a great road

that leads somewhere.

Before me,

there is no path.

Behind me,

a path is made.

The path is nothing more

than the trace my own feet leave.

That is why

I find myself, always,

standing at the far edge of the path.

What a crooked path—

a path of twists and wandering.

That path nearly vanished through self-abandonment.

That path once shut in by despair.

That path ground down by the agonies of youth.

Turning back,

my path is worthy of a shudder.

Who would look upon this scene—

scattered,

out of joint—

and believe it the path of the living.

And yet,

this is the path, and no other,

that has brought me to this life.

And so,

here I ended up.

Looking upon this ravaged path,

I am brought to tears

by the breadth of nature's compassion.

From that path—

reckless as it was—

nature showed me clearly

what it means to live.

The one who dragged me around,

as it looked the other way,

and just as I thought I had arrived,

the one who cried out:

Wake. Wake—

was nature.

This was truly

the love of a stern father.

I came, slowly and deeply, to appreciate

the significance of fully

becoming a child.

For never letting go

of nature's hand,

at last, I grasped myself.

Just then,

suddenly everything changed.

In an instant, what stood before me radiated light,

and the sky and the earth, as if boiling over, broke into motion.

At that moment,

nature parted from my hands,

leaving me a gentle smile,

and faded toward the eternal horizon.

Thus,

its profound presence

filled and permeated the universe.

My soul, shocked,

is then pierced, suddenly, with a voice:

Walk.

I gathered myself and felt the weight

of the dutiful fate, press through my entire body

— the child's duty!

My shoulders grew heavy;

and so, I no longer had the hands of support.

The hand,

subconsciously depended on,

was no longer there.

All I must do is trust the father

that fills and permeates this universe

and throw my entirety.

I experienced the inability to walk,

not even the first step.

For quite a long while,

as cold and oily sweat ran down,

I stayed, in one spot.

Drawing my heart together,

I laid my hand upon father's chest.

Then,

my feet began to move on their own.

Interestingly I came to achieve

a certain state of self

taking hold of itself.

I no longer think

how to go.

I no longer think

which path to take.

Before me lay endless beds of rock

filling the entire view.

Between them, flowers bloom as water flows.

Rocks are present and cliffs hang.

Each and all, lively.

I just walk according to the command

of that mysterious drive that has taken hold.

Yet— how eerily silent it is, all around.

At times,

I fear ending up at the edge of the world.

Loneliness is painful, like the deaf.

Once more, in those moments,

I pray to father.

Between the slightest gap of that view,

father shows others walking resolute

in the same direction.

I tremble at the human instinct

to rejoice in one's own kind,

and raise my voice to express my blessing.

Then, before the eternal horizon,

I draw a breath that opens my chest.

As my eyes widen,

the land, from all directions,

reveals its parts clearly to me.

In the shade of thick grass,

small humans could also be seen

crawling in swarms.

They, and also I,

are a part

of the larger whole called humanity.

Yet humanity does not hesitate

to let what is without use to rot away.

Humans are salmon eggs.

As long as even one hundred remain out of ten million,

humanity will never perish.

Nature, for humanity,

foresees this decay,

and creates humans in abundance.

Let what shall rot,

rot.

Those who defy nature

all rot.

For now,

I cannot concern myself with them.

I must be nurtured and cultivated further by this land

and let myself

grow into myself.

The child holds within

a burning desire to return father's deep compassion.

O,

how far the journey is for humanity.

And,

that great road does not exist.

Children of nature

must make the way with all their strength.

Walk on, walk on.

Whatever comes forth,

surmount it and walk on.

Step forward into this radiant land.

Before me,

there is no path.

Behind me,

a path is made.

O, father—

Father who set me on my own.

Do not take your eyes from me

and see to my protection.

Fill me, always,

with your profound presence.

For the sake

of this long journey.

About TAKAMURA Kōtarō

Kōtarō Takamura (1883–1956): a poet and sculptor. He studied art in Europe and the United States, where he came under the influence of Auguste Rodin. After returning to Japan, he published the essay The Green Sun in 1910, in which he wrote, "Even if someone paints the sun green, I will not call it wrong." This declaration of the artist's absolute freedom of individuality and subjectivity startled young artists of the time and marked a turning point in modern Japanese art. Unbound by established authority or convention, he sought to carve out his own path by following his inner voice. The spirit expressed in Journey (Dōtei) reflects that conviction.

However, during the Pacific War he published many poems in cooperation with the war effort and later carried deep remorse for them. After the war, he withdrew to a mountain hut in Hanamaki, Iwate Prefecture, where he lived alone for seven years. In that severe environment, without electricity or gas, he referred to this period as self-imposed exile (jiko-ryūtaku). It was a solitary atonement he chose precisely because he understood, more than anyone, the weight of his own words.
